About

A socioecological and developmental approach to human behaviors.

I am a behavioral and developmental researcher with a PhD in Educational Psychology and a specialization in Human Development. My work examines how people decide what information to trust, when to revise an existing belief, and whether to continue relying on another person when outcomes are uncertain.

I became interested in these questions by studying the immediate context of an interaction. Seemingly small features—whether a speaker is visible, how a message is delivered, whether someone feels directly addressed, or which social cues are available—can change how people interpret information and respond to the person providing it. In my research, I have examined how communication channels, source characteristics, and prior beliefs shape belief revision, disagreement, engagement, and persistence.

Over time, my questions have expanded beyond the immediate interaction. I am also interested in how people’s decisions are shaped by the broader social and ecological environments in which they live. Experiences such as residential mobility, group membership, community relationships, cultural expectations, educational settings, and access to resources can influence which information people encounter, whom they learn from, and which strategies are realistic or useful within their circumstances. I therefore approach behavior at multiple levels, from cues present in a single conversation to the wider marco-environmental conditions surrounding that conversation

Across these levels, I am especially interested in variation. I do not only want to know whether an effect exists. I want to understand why the same experience may lead to different decisions among people, across developmental stages, and in different environments. This perspective has shaped my research on children’s trust and learning, social identity, residential mobility, motivation, and effort investment. Methodologically, I have led experiments, surveys, behavioral studies, and evidence syntheses across the full research lifecycle. My work has involved developing research questions, designing materials, recruiting and moderating participants, protecting human subjects, building datasets, selecting appropriate quantitative models, and communicating findings. I try to choose methods that fit the decision being studied rather than forcing every question into the same research design or outcome measure.

Although my training is academic, I also care deeply about where the evidence goes next. I enjoy translating theoretical questions into practical research decisions that can inform products, educational programs, interventions, and communication experiences. At the same time, I believe responsible research requires being clear about what a study establishes, what remains uncertain, and what should be tested before claiming broader impact.

Ultimately, I study how people make sense of information within the realities of their environments. Whether I am examining a communication cue in a brief interaction or a broader social condition that develops over time, my goal is the same: to produce careful behavioral evidence that helps explain not only what people decide, but why those decisions vary across contexts..

I study how people make decisions—from the immediate cues within an interaction to the broader social and ecological environments that shape their choices.

Research approach

From question to evidence to action

I lead research across the full lifecycle while selecting methods that fit the decision—not the other way around.

Compare Across Development

Compare multiple age groups to examine whether an observed behavioral pattern appears similar across the developmental periods sampled or varies with age. Because age-related differences may reflect several developing capacities—including intention and promise understanding, attention regulation, and self-regulation—these comparisons are treated as evidence of developmental variation rather than proof of a specific mechanism unless those capacities are measured directly (Atance & Jackson, 2009; Chin & Lin, 2018; Kanngiesser et al., 2017).

Connect Context to Mechanism

Use a socioecological approach that begins by documenting whether differences are associated with features of people’s social and physical environments. I then use controlled experiments to move beyond group comparisons and test which mechanisms may explain those differences (Oishi, 2014).

Translate Evidence Carefully

Separate descriptive patterns, associations, causal findings, and speculative interpretations. I then identify what the evidence may suggest, what it cannot suggest, and what it implies for educational programs, interventions, or a specific theory development. I also clarify what still needs to be tested in the future for deepening our practical and theoretical knowledge.
